# 1. The Art of Learning: A Human Analogy

Understanding how an AI works is understanding how intelligence itself is shaped. 

When you were a baby, you didn't know what a cat was, how to walk, or how to say "potato." Intelligence isn't a magic trick pre-installed in our heads. It works like a construction site: you need bricks to build a wall, and those bricks are **Information (DATA)**. 


## 1.1 Information (Data):

Before we understand better the learning, we need to understand how the world works. **Nothing in the world happens in isolation. Every result has a "Web of Information" behind it, and each piece of information has a weight**

**Information:** In other words, to discover the result a information alone has limited meaning; real understanding comes from combining multiple pieces of information

- $NewInformation = Information1 + Information2 + Information3$

* Let's take an example: we want to know if a person will cancel their subscription on a streaming site. Just one piece of information is useless in this case, so we would need to know several things (several pieces of information), such as LastLogin, UsageTime, Contract, and more. This, combining the information: $$CancelSubscription = LastLogin (Information1) + UsageTime (Information2) + Contract (Information3)$$

* In other words, to discover the result (whether or not they will cancel the subscription), we need several pieces of information. This was just one example, but 99% of things in our world work this way: several pieces of information together.

**The Weights:** However, not all information has the same importance, but each one has a weight in the final decision. That is, we have more relevant information than others. Other examples, in the previous one:

* Knowing that the client hasn't logged in for 2 days (LastLogin) is information, but it's much less important than knowing that their contract expires today (Contract). Therefore, the "weight" of the contract on the scale is much greater.

* Our calculation is not a simple sum, but a Weighted Sum: $$CancelSubscription = (LastLogin\cdot WeightLastLogin) + (UsageTime \cdot WeightUsageTime) + (Contract \cdot WeightContract)$$ 


```

SITUATION: Imagine you are crossing the street and gathering various pieces of information to decide whether to CROSS or NOT CROSS:

Information 1: The color of the oncoming car (Red).
Information 2: The car model (Sedan).
Information 3: The car's speed (80 km/h).
Information 4: The car's distance (10 meters).

So we can desribe the situation in a math formula

Cross/Stay = Color + Model + Speed + Distance

For your brain to arrive at the New Information (decision to "Cross" or "Not Cross/Stay"), it assigns automatic weights:

1. The information "Color" and "Model" receive an almost Zero Weight; they are useless for your decision at that moment.

2. The information "Speed" and "Distance" receive Maximum Weight; they are the information that truly "determines" the outcome (whether you should cross or not).

So now the formula changes

Cross/Stay = (Color * WeightColor) + (Model * WeightModel) + (Speed * WeightSpeed) + (Distance * WeightDistance)

Cross/Stay = (Color * 0.1) + (Model * 0.1) + (Speed * 0.4) + (Distance * 0.4)

```

#### **EXAMPLES**

1. **The Price of a House**: A price doesn't just "appear" on a tag. It is the final result of a connection between multiple inputs:
      - Size $m^2$ (information 1) + Number of Rooms (information 2) + Location (information 3) = Price (result).

      * **The Weight about information: In the real world, Location usually pulls the "Price" much harder than the Number of Rooms. A small house at the beach might be more expensive than a mansion in the middle of nowhere.**
      
      * **If you change the Location, the Price moves.**
      * **If you increase the m², the Price follows.**
      
          The AI's job: Discover the relationship between the information and the result, and calculate exactly how much weight each piece of information has on the final outcome.

2. **Winning a Football Match**: Who wins a game isn't random. It’s a complex relationship between different data points:

      - Players' Skill (information 1) + Team Strategy (information 2) + Home Crowd Support (information 3) = Match Winner (result).

      * **The Weight: While the Crowd helps, Players' Skill and Strategy are the heavy-duty (like if you have an Neymar on your team, the players skills contribute more for the result, than the crow helps)**

      * **Even if you have the best players, if the Strategy data is low, the Winner result changes.**


         The AI's job: Analyze thousands of past matches to see which combination of these factors most likely leads to a victory.


3. **The Recommendation Engine**: Why does Netflix or TikTok seem to know exactly what you want to watch? It’s not magic; it’s the relationship between your time and your taste.

      - What you clicked (information 1)+ How long you watched (information 2) + What people like you watched (information 3) = Your Next Favorite Show (result).
      
      * **The Weight: Watch Time is a much heavier string than a Click.**

      * **The Connection: If you watched a Action Movie (Data A) until the very end, and skipped a Comedy (Data B) in the first 2 minutes, the relationship is clear: Your Interest is pulled toward Action.**

         The AI's Job: It looks at millions of other users. It finds the pattern: "Users who finished Movie X also loved Movie Y." It connects your history to the history of others to predict your future.



So information alone is just a "brick." It’s static. Intelligence happens when you realize that one brick is related to another. **Everything is a Relationship**

> The Secret: Learning is simply the process of discovering these relationships between the informations and how much each information contribute for the result.
--- 

So the logic is always the same: **you try to find the relationship in the data (how this become that?) and the weights for each information, you fail, you adjust the error, and you try again.** You do this thousands of times until you stop making mistakes. And BOOM—you’ve learned something.


We can define three ways in which humans learn:


## 1.2 TYPES OF LEARNING 

### 1. Studying Predefined Patterns (The Student with the Answer Key)
Here, intelligence works through **association**. You learn because someone who already holds the knowledge shows you what is right and what is wrong. So **here you already know the relationship between data and the weights for each information**

* **The Doctor Example (Diagnosis):** Imagine a student analyzing a thousand X-ray exams. On each one, a veteran professor has written: *"This is pneumonia"* or *"This is a healthy lung."*
* **What happens in the mind:** The student didn't invent the disease; they are studying the relationship provided by the professor. They start to notice that every time the label is "pneumonia," there is a specific gray shadow in the image. They memorize this pattern to repeat the diagnosis in the future.


---

### 2. Recognizing Patterns (The Observational Detective)
Here, intelligence works through **grouping (clustering)**. No one gives you the answer; you identify what repeats and conclude that those things belong to the same category. **It's impossible to know the relationship between something without ever having seen it before, so here you don’t have labels, so instead of learning exact relationships, you discover patterns and similarities.**

* **The Business Building Example:** Imagine you are standing in front of a commercial building. You observe that one group of people always arrives in black cars, wears suits, carries briefcases, and talks on the phone in a hurry. Another group arrives by bicycle, wearing t-shirts and headphones.
* **What happens in the mind:** No one told you who is who. But you **recognize the pattern**: the group in suits repeats and seems to hold important or leadership positions. You created a mental "folder" for "Business People" just by observing the repetition of clothes and behaviors.

---

### 3 Senses (The Master of Calibration)
This is learning through **reaction**. It is the intelligence of practice, where the error is not a failure, but the very information you needed to adjust your movement. **Here you don't have the answer and you're not just looking at what already exists; you create your own relationship and weights through trial and error.**

* **The Chef Example (Adjusting the Seasoning):** Imagine you are inventing a new sauce. You add too much pepper (**Action**). You taste it, and your tongue burns (**Sense/Error**). Your brain registers: *"Too much pepper ruined the plane."* On the next try, you add a spoonful of honey. You taste it and feel that the flavor is balanced (**Sense/Success**).
* **What happens in the mind:** You are **calibrating**. You didn't follow a ready-made recipe (study), and you didn't just stand there watching (recognition). You acted, felt the result, and adjusted the error. With every "burn" or "good taste," your intelligence turns an internal screw to reach perfection.

---


### 💡 PRO TIP: The Hybrid Architect Strategy
> **This is where the magic happens.** You don't have to choose just one method; the most efficient way to learn is to **organize the chaos** before trying to assign a value to it.
>
> **The Prospector’s Strategy:** Imagine you find a mountain of unknown stones.
> 1.  **First, Group (1.2):** You separate the stones by color and texture. You’ve created "folders" without knowing their names yet.
> 2.  **Then, Label (1.1):** You take just **one** stone from each group to an expert. He tells you: *"This is gold, this is pyrite."*
>
> **The Result:** You just discovered the value (weights) of thousands of items at once. By grouping first, you optimize your brain to understand the relationship of the whole by only studying the parts that matter (So here you combine Studying Predefined Patterns and Recognize Patterns).


### **IMPORTANT NOTE (The Dependency Principle)**
> No intelligence—human or artificial—comes from thin air.

One of the biggest mistakes people make is thinking that AI (or even human intelligence) is magic. It isn’t. **Intelligence is a processor, not a creator of facts.**

To understand this, think about these two scenarios:

**1. You can't predict what you've never seen (Supervised)**

- The Mushroom Example: Imagine you find a strange, purple mushroom in the woods. Is it poisonous? You can be the smartest person on Earth, but if you have never seen that mushroom or know the weights about mushrooms before or read a book about it, it is impossible to know. * The AI Parallel: You cannot ask an AI to predict house prices if it has never seen a list of house prices. Without the "Answer Key", the machine has no "past" to build a "future."

**2. You can't calibrate without a reaction (Reinforcement)**
- The Cooking Example: You can't learn how much salt a soup needs if you don't have the sense of taste. If you can't "feel" the result of your action, you are just guessing in the dark.

 ## 2.2 The types of Learning

 How we discuss **The humans have 3 types of learning**, in machine learning we 'copy' this method:

Let's see one per one, with more details and informations:

### Supervised Learning
#### 1. The Core Logic: Response as a Composition

In manufacturing, a **NewInformation** is never an isolated fact. It is the outcome of several **Individual Informations** interacting with one another.

In nature, not all information carries the same relevance. To represent this, we assign **Weights (w)** to each input. The raw formula the model attempts to solve is:

$$NewInformation = (Information_1 \cdot weightInfo_1) + (Information_2 \cdot weightInfo_2) + (Information_n \cdot weightInfo_n)$$

* **New Information (Final Response):** The combination of individual information
* **Information:** A simple data (Temperature, Material Metric, etc.).
* **Weights:** The "degree of influence" each piece of information has on the final result (NewInformation).

> NOTE: We can say that $NewInformation$ it's a function, cuz is defined as a rule that relates each element of a set
---

#### 2. The "Study" Phase: Reversing the Equation

The crucial difference between traditional software and Machine Learning lies in who defines the weights:
* **Traditional Software:** A human programmer manually defines the weights/rules.
* **Supervised Learning:** We provide the NewInformation and the raw Information (where the weights are "invisible" but present). The computer then performs the inverse calculation.

> "The model studies past examples to discover and calculate which specific weights ($w$) allow the sum of information to match the real-world response as closely as possible."



---

#### 3. The Goal: Analyzing and Pruning Weights

The "raw" essence of this Learning was **studying the weights** themselves. We forced the algorithms (LinearRegression, DecisionTree, RandomForest and more) to reveal which pieces of information were important or not

* **Noise Detection:** If an information (like Pressure) receives a weight near zero, it is discarded as noise, it's a useless information for our model.
* **Signal Identification:** If an information (like Temperature) receives a high weight, it is identified as the primary driver of quality.

So here to study the weights we **NEED** to have the $NewInformation$, like we see in the **The Dependency Principle**, and how we see in the table form it's necessary to have the column, without that it's impossible to know the relationship of weights.

#### 4. Types: Regression and classification

When we define that $NewInformation$ is the result of a combination of data ($IndividualInformation$ and the $Weights$ for each individual information), we are actually creating a Mathematical Function. And in mathematics, every function has a visual representation in space, a line (or plane, or hyperplane). 
- The Decision Line: Imagine that each piece of information (Feature) is a dimension. If you have only one piece of information, the model draws a Line. If he has two, he draws a Plan. If there are thousands, he draws a Hyperplane (something we can't see, but which mathematics calculates perfectly).

Mathematically, the values of the unknowns — which we call **weights** — determine the slope and position of this line; in other words, they control whether the line goes up faster, slower, or even goes down.

So, in simple terms, what we do is adjust the weights to control how the line behaves, changing its slope and position until it fits the data as well as possible.

1. **Regression:** When the target is a continuous/decimal number (1.23, 100.45, etc.), our goal here is to adjust the line so that it is at a shorter distance from all points. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regression_analysis)

2. **Classification:** When the target is a class, our goal here is to create a line that separates the different existing classes. (https://medium.com/@aakash013/master-supervised-learning-with-top-classification-techniques-af870f710c82)


In summary, the **main focus** is **study and optimize the weights to find the better values that solve the problem**, because they completely determine how the line is positioned and their slope.

> NOTE: Solve the problem, in this context, is an optimization problem: it is about finding the optimal function, adjusting the weights (unknowns) until they define the line that best describes the data.

### Unsupervised Learning

#### 1. The Logic of Hidden Structures

If Supervised Learning is about **studying the weights** to hit a specific target, Unsupervised Learning is about **discovering the natural shape** of the data when no target ($NewInformation$) exists. 

In this mode, we don't provide the "$NewInformation$" or an answer key so it's impossible to know whereabouts of weights. So here the goal change:

* **The Goal:** To find/separe/create our own  **$NewInformation$** (target)

The goal is not to create a true target, but to discover hidden structure in the data (such as clusters and patterns). For intuition, we can think of these structures as ‘pseudo-targets’, but unlike supervised learning, they are not ground-truth labels, they are inferred from the data itself.
#### 2. Pattern Recognition (The Weight of Similarity)

The model calculates the "mathematical distance" between data points. 
- If 500 samples have high Temperature and high Material Fusion, the model groups them into a "Folder." 
- It doesn't know this folder means "High Quality"; it only knows that these samples share a **relationship**.


### Reinforcement Learning

#### 1. The Logic of Cause and Effect

In Reinforcement Learning, the model learns by **interacting** rather than observing a static table. We don't have a fixed $NewInformation$ (Target) ready; the model creates its own experience through a loop of action and reaction.

* **The Agent:** The "student" (the algorithm).
* **The Action:** The "Information" the agent decides to generate/execute.
* **The Reward:** This is our "Feedback". It’s a signal telling the model if the action was good (+1) or bad (-1).

#### 2. The Weight of Strategy (Policy)



In RL, the "Weights" are assigned to **Decisions** in a sequence. 
* The model asks: "What is the weight (importance) of this specific action to reach the final goal?"
* **The Goal:** To maximize the **Cumulative Reward**. The model discovers which sequence of "Information/Actions" leads to the best final result.

#### 3. The Goal: Discovering Weights through Trial and Error

In this mode, the **Agent builds its own table**:
1. **Exploration:** It tries an action.
2. **Reward:** It receives a positive or negative signal.
3. **Adjustment:** It updates the "Weight" of that behavior. 

If Supervised Learning is "learning from a teacher," Reinforcement Learning is **"learning from consequences."**

## 3.1 Understanding Models

First, let's differentiate two key concepts:
1. **Machine Learning:** These are the studies, calculations, and steps taken to achieve learning (the process).
2. **Model:** This is the already trained algorithm. It is the "file" that contains the extracted knowledge. For example:
    - If it was **Supervised** learning, the model has already stored the **Ideal Weights**.
    - If it was **Unsupervised**, it has already stored the **Pattern Map**.
    - If it was **Reinforcement** learning, it has already stored the **Best Strategy**.

In other words, the model is the final result: a mathematical structure that simulated the phases of human learning and now **retains** that knowledge. We can have several specialized models:
- **Model 1:** Predicts the price of a house.
- **Model 2:** Recognizes large animals.
- **Model 3:** Identifies if a photo is of a cat or a dog.

> **In simpler terms:** Machine Learning is the course the computer attended; the Model is the diploma and the knowledge it carries forward.

---

## 3.2 Types of Models

Many people divide models into two large families based on **how** they find the "best line" (the ideal weights). This is one of the most important divisions in the field:
1. **Non-Gradient Models**
2. **Gradient-Based Models**

### Workflow and Learning
As we previously discussed the types of learning, it is important to understand that a model—whether it uses gradients or not—can perform any type of learning: Supervised, Unsupervised, or Reinforcement. What changes between model types is the **form and logic (algorithm)** through which it obtains results (correct weights, patterns, etc.).

These two large families (With and Without Gradient) have smaller sub-groups and specific algorithms that we will cover in the future, but understanding this difference in "personality" is the first step to choosing the right tool for each problem.

### Sub-groups of each family:
To make your journey easier, here are the most famous names you will find in each "family":

**1. NON-GRADIENT Models:**
- Decision Trees
- Random Forests
- Ensembles (Part I)
- SVMs (Support Vector Machines)

**2. GRADIENT-BASED Models:**
- Linear Regression
- Logistic Regression
- Ensembles (Part II)
- Neural Networks

---

## 3.3 Summary: The Model as the "Mind" of the Operation

To leave no room for doubt: if Machine Learning is the training (the act of going to the gym), the **Model** is the fit body (the result).

People and companies use models to "package" intelligence. But before reaching the ready-made model, data scientists look at the problem and decide which "family" and "training type" to use:

**The Choice of Training (The "How"):**
- If I have the answers (labels/ground truth), I use **Supervised** training.
- If I only have a mountain of messy data, I use **Unsupervised** training.
- If I need the machine to learn how to play or navigate an environment, I use **Reinforcement** learning.

**The Choice of Family (The "Logic"):**
- For problems where the adjustment needs to be fine and constant (like photos or voices), we choose the **Gradient-Based** family (e.g., Neural Networks).
- For problems with clear logic, rules, and tables (like credit approval or inventory), the **Non-Gradient** family (e.g., Decision Trees) is usually faster and more accurate.

### The Ready-Made Model (Knowledge Retention)
Once the training and family are defined, the result is the Model. It is the algorithm that "froze" the ideal weights.
- It no longer needs to study.
- It no longer needs to make mistakes.
- It simply receives new information and, using its "mathematical memory," provides the answer.

**Moral of the story:** The Model is the specialist you created. You choose the correct training so that, in the end, the Model knows exactly how to act when facing the unknown.

> **Golden Tip:** Models are reusable. Once you have trained a model to identify cats, you can send it anywhere in the world and it will continue to know what a cat is, without needing the internet or new examples. It is knowledge transformed into a tool.
