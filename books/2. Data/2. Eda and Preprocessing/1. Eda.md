## 1. Data Overview
The goal here is to understand the data. In many cases, we receive a dataset without context, only with a problem to solve. Therefore, we must first understand its structure and what it represents.

### Shape
**The shape of a dataset refers to its structural dimensions, representing the number of elements along each axis (rows, columns, and higher dimensions)**

### Data Types
**While Python has standard types like int, float, and str, libraries like NumPy and Pandas use more specific types to optimize memory and processing speed. The data type tells the computer how to interpret the binary stored in memory—whether it should treat a value as a number to be added, a category to be grouped, or a date to be sequenced.**

`.dtypes` for all the dataste and `.dtype` for a single on column

### Data Samples 
**A sample (also known as an observation, record, or instance) represents a single unit of data in your collection. In a standard 2D dataset, each row is a sample, while each column represents a feature (a characteristic of that sample). For example, in your "Drugs Project," a single sample would represent one specific patient, including their age, sex, and the drug they were prescribed.**

`.head()` View the first N samples

`.tail()` View the last N samples

### Column Names
**Column names provide the essential mapping between raw data and human-readable features. In Machine Learning, these names represent your Independent Variables (features like Age, BMI, or Blood_Pressure) and your Dependent Variable (the Target or Label you want to predict, like Drug_Type). Clear and consistent naming is the first step in successful data cleaning, as it allows you to select, filter, and manipulate specific data points without confusion.**

**A Constant Feature is a column in your dataset where every single row contains the exact same value. In terms of information theory, these features have zero variance. ## 4. Eda for Data Engineering
We already know that EDA is the stage where we analyze the data and look for defects.

This section sits between EDA and preprocessing. Here, we are not just detecting problems, we are analyzing the data with the intention of fixing it.

Our goal is to understand the problems in a way that directly guides how we will preprocess the data.

### Features Types (Encoders)
#### 1. The Problem (Non-Numerical Data)
In real-world datasets, not all **Features** are numerical. Many of them are **categorical**, meaning they represent labels or categories rather than quantities.
Examples:
- Country: "Brazil", "Portugal", "USA"
- Color: "Red", "Blue", "Green"
- Gender: "Male", "Female"
The issue is that **machine learning models only understand numbers**.  
We cannot perform mathematical operations like:
> "Brazil" × Weight 
So, before feeding this data into a model, we must **translate these categories into numbers** — without destroying their meaning.
---

#### 2. What Encoders Do
Encoders are techniques used to **convert categorical (non-numerical) data into numerical representations**.
Their goal is to:
- Make data understandable for algorithms
- Preserve as much information as possible
- Avoid introducing misleading relationships
Think of encoders as **translators**:
> They convert human language (categories) into machine language (numbers)

---

#### 3. Choose the best encoder
Choosing the right encoder is not arbitrary — it depends on the **type of data** and the **meaning behind the categories**.
There is no universal “best” encoder. The goal is to **preserve information without introducing bias or false relationships**.
1. Is there a natural order in the categories? (So the feature have a order -> Ordinal)
2. Is there NO order between categories? (So the feature dont' have a order -> Nominal)

---

### Sensitibility (Scalers)

#### 1. The Problem (Different Scales)
In real-world datasets, even when all Features are numerical, they often exist on **very different scales**.
Examples:
- Age: 18 → 90
- Salary: 1,000 → 100,000
- Distance: 0.5 → 10
The issue is that machine learning models are sensitive to the **magnitude** of values.
If we directly use this data:
- Large numbers (like Salary) can **dominate** the learning process
- Smaller numbers (like Age) may become almost irrelevant
> This creates an unfair scenario where some Features "bully" others.

---

#### 2. What Scalers Do
Scalers are techniques used to **adjust the range of numerical features** so that they are on a similar scale.
Their goal is to:
- Ensure all Features contribute fairly
- Improve model performance and stability
- Speed up training (especially for gradient-based models)
Think of scalers as **standardizers**:
> They make all "bricks" the same size before building the wall

---
#### 3. Choose the best scaler
Choosing the right scaler depends on the **distribution of your data**, the **presence of outliers**, and the **type of model** you are using.
There is no single best scaler — the goal is to **make features comparable without distorting their meaning**.
1. Does your data have significant outliers?
2. Is your data relatively clean and normally distributed?
3. Do you need values strictly bounded (e.g., between 0 and 1)?
4. Are you working with sparse data?


---
### Time Series (Temporal Features)

#### 1. The Problem (Time is not just a number)
In many real-world datasets, **time itself is a Feature**. It often appears as a string, such as:
- "2024-01-01"
- "03/15/2023"
- "July 10, 2022"
At first glance, this looks like simple data — but it hides a deeper complexity.
 Problems:
- The model **cannot understand strings**
- Time has an **order and continuity** (yesterday → today → tomorrow)
- Dates contain **multiple hidden features** (day, month, year, weekday, season)
 So even though time *looks simple*, it carries **structured and sequential information**.

---
#### 2. What We Do With Time Features
We transform raw time data into formats that models can understand while preserving its meaning.

---
#####  Step 1: Convert to Datetime
Before anything, we convert strings into a **datetime format**.
Example:
- "2024-01-01" → `datetime`
 Why?
- Allows extraction of components
- Enables sorting and time-based operations

---
#####  Step 2: Extract Useful Features
From a single date, we can create multiple Features:
- Year
- Month
- Day
- Day of the week
- Is weekend (0 or 1)
 One column becomes many informative "bricks"

---
#####  Step 3: Respect Time Order (The Core Rule)
In time series problems, **order matters**.
 Unlike normal datasets:
- You CANNOT shuffle the data
- You must train on **past data** and predict the **future**
Example:
- Train: 2018 → 2022  
- Test: 2023  
 The model must learn from the past to predict what comes next.

---
#####  Step 4: Recency Matters
In time series, **recent data is usually more important than old data**.
Examples:
- Stock prices
- Weather
- User behavior
 A pattern from yesterday is often more relevant than one from 5 years ago.
This introduces concepts like:
- Rolling windows
- Moving averages
- Lag features (previous values)

---
#### 3. Types of Time Series
Time series data can take different forms depending on structure and complexity:

---
##### 3.1 Univariate Time Series
Only one variable changes over time.
Example:
- Daily temperature
- Stock price of one company
 Focus: how one value evolves over time

---
##### 3.2 Multivariate Time Series
Multiple variables evolve together over time.
Example:
- Temperature, humidity, and wind speed
- Stock price + trading volume
 Focus: relationships between variables over time

---
##### 3.3 Stationary vs Non-Stationary
- **Stationary:** Statistical properties (mean, variance) stay constant over time  
- **Non-Stationary:** Trends, seasonality, or changing variance  
 Most real-world data is **non-stationary**, which makes modeling harder

---
##### 3.4 Seasonal Time Series
Patterns repeat at regular intervals.
Examples:
- Sales increase every December
- Traffic peaks during weekdays

> In time series, we are not just learning patterns — we are learning how patterns evolve over time.


## 5. Eda for Data Tunning
This stage focuses on refining both the **Data** and the **Algorithm** to extract maximum predictive power.


### Feature Selection
The goal is to provide the model with only the cleanest signals, removing noise that can cause false patterns or overfitting.
---

### **WHY?**
1.  **Reduced Overfitting:** Less redundant data means less chance of the model making decisions based on "noise" or statistical coincidences.
2.  **Improved Accuracy:** By removing irrelevant columns (e.g., "eye color" to predict diabetes), the model focuses on the true drivers of the target.
3.  **Speed & Efficiency:** Models with 5 columns train and run much faster than models with 500. This saves memory, processing power, and money.
4.  **Multicollinearity Removal:** Avoids having two columns saying the same thing, which can confuse linear models.

### Reduce Dimensionality
The goal is to condense information from many features into fewer new variables, preserving the data's essence while reducing computational cost, improving correlation, and enabling better visualization.

* **WHY USE IT?**
1. **Information Compression:** Transforms complex data (like MNIST's 784 pixels) into a few components without losing the primary patterns.
2. **Visualization:** Reduces data to 2D or 3D, making it possible to see clusters and groupings.
3. **Noise Removal:** Helps the model focus on the directions where the data actually varies, discarding irrelevant details.
